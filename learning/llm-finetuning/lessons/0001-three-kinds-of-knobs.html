Three kinds of knobs

What your fine-tune actually does, and which settings can move AUC at all.

The win for this lesson: you can take any line of your training config and say which of three jobs it does. That tells you whether changing it could raise AUC.

Predict first (10 seconds, no peeking)

You have run more than a dozen experiments: three model sizes, two prompt formats, a new seed, two LoRA ranks, early-season games. How many of them moved validation log-loss by more than your 0.002 win threshold?

Answer: none. Every LLM run and XGBoost land between 0.628 and 0.630 (docs/plans/llm-finetune-handoff.md §2). Keep that in mind while you read the rest.

1. What your model is really doing

Each game becomes a ~280-token text table (streaks, rest days, adjusted win% over the last 3–21 games, travel), ending with the line Home margin =. The model is trained to write the margin, e.g. +3.

stats prompt→ Llama-3.2-1B + LoRA→ P(+) vs P(-)→ p_home→ Platt→ log-loss · AUC

The number of points barely matters for scoring. What gets scored is the first token. For game 22100230, E11 gave + a probability of 0.622 and - 0.377, so p_home = 0.622 / (0.622 + 0.377) = 0.625. The home team won by 3.

Because of that, your LLM is a binary classifier dressed as a text generator. Two settings make it work like one:

  • train_on_completion_only: true: the loss counts only the answer tokens, not the 280-token prompt. Otherwise the sign would carry almost none of the gradient.
  • Platt recalibration: before it, your fine-tunes pick home about 70% of the time, against a true rate of 55%. Platt fits a logistic curve on validation to correct that lean[sklearn].

AUC measures ranking, not calibration. AUC asks one thing: when you compare a game the home team won with one it lost, does the winner get the higher p_home? Platt is a monotonic rescaling, so it keeps that order, and AUC stays where it was. Platt improves log-loss and accuracy. To raise AUC, the model has to rank games better.

2. Every knob does one of three jobs

Each line in configs/train_llm/qlora/_defaults.yaml and the model files does one of three things:

You raised the effective batch from 8 to 32 because 78% of steps were hitting the gradient clip, and you cut training to one epoch because the second one added nothing. 3. The test that tells the jobs apart

If 1B, 3B and 8B LLMs, plus a gradient-boosted tree that works nothing like an LLM, all reach the same log-loss on the same inputs, then model capacity and the training method are not what limits them. The inputs are. TabLLM, the main study on fine-tuning LLMs on rows of a table written out as text, found the same pattern. LLMs help most when labeled data is scarce. With plenty of rows, gradient-boosted trees catch up[TabLLM]. You have 23,000 games, which is plenty.

4. What else you can do, ranked

  1. Add new information (an information job). Injuries and player availability are allowed, and none of your current features cover them. A team missing its star can look identical to a full-strength team in every stat you have. This is the only lever your evidence points to. It is a data project, not a training change.
  2. Blend the LLM with XGBoost (N2, CPU only, free). Averaging helps only when two models make different mistakes. Your two LLM seeds correlate at 0.993, and averaging them gained nothing. Check the LLM–XGBoost correlation first.
  3. One cheap learning-job check: LoRA on all layers. Your target_modules list only the attention projections (q/k/v/o). The current best evidence, LoRA Without Regret, found that attention-only LoRA underperforms LoRA that includes the MLP layers, even when both train the same number of parameters[TM]. It takes 21 minutes on Colab. Given section 3, expect a tie, but it is the one learning knob where outside evidence goes against your current setting.
  4. E8, the learning curve. Train on 10%, 25% and 50% of the data. If 25% already scores like 100%, more seasons of the same stats won't help, and that strengthens the case for item 1.

Platt is monotonic, so every game keeps its rank and AUC stays put. It shifts the threshold, which corrects the home lean and helps accuracy. The ≤0.003 differences in your §2 table come from cross-fitting, where each fold gets slightly different Platt parameters.
